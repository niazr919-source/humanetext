Most people learn to paraphrase by rewriting a sentence one word at a time. Open the source, read a line, swap a few words for synonyms, move a clause, move on.

That method does not produce a paraphrase. It produces a slightly disguised copy, and it is the single most common reason honest students end up in a misconduct meeting.

## Why synonym-swapping fails

There is a name for it: **patchwriting**. The term comes from composition research and describes text built by substituting words into someone else's sentence structure.

> **Original:** The rapid expansion of urban populations in the twentieth century placed unprecedented strain on municipal water systems.

> **Patchwritten:** The quick growth of city populations in the 1900s put unmatched pressure on local water networks.

Every content word has changed. The sentence is still the original author's. Word order, clause arrangement, the choice of what to foreground, the rhythm of the sentence — all inherited.

Two separate problems follow. **It is still plagiarism**, because structure is part of what an author produced. And **it is detectable**, because similarity checkers match sequences, and patchwriting leaves enough word order intact to register.

The deeper issue is that it does not require understanding. You can patchwrite a passage on a subject you know nothing about, which is exactly why it is not accepted as your own work.

## The method that works

Four steps. The important one is the second.

**1. Read the passage until you understand it.** Not until you have read it — until you could explain it to someone. If you cannot, you are not ready to paraphrase it, and no amount of rewording will disguise that.

**2. Put it out of sight.** Close the tab. Turn the book over. This step is the whole method, and skipping it is why people fail. You cannot rebuild a sentence in your own structure while the original is in front of you, because your brain will edit rather than rebuild.

**3. Write what it said from memory.** In your own words, at whatever length it takes. It will come out differently, because you are reconstructing an idea rather than transforming a sentence. It may come out longer or shorter. Both are fine.

**4. Check it against the original.** Now open it again. You are checking two things: whether you got the meaning right, and whether any distinctive phrasing survived by accident. Fix both. If the author used a term of art you need, put it in quotation marks.

This feels slower than editing line by line. It is faster in practice, because you write once rather than nervously reworking the same sentence.

## The four-word test

A rough working rule, used in some form by many institutions: **no run of more than about four consecutive words in common with the source**, unless it is quoted.

It is not a legal threshold and nobody counts on their fingers. It is useful as a self-check. Read your paragraph beside the original and look for surviving runs. Common phrases — *on the other hand*, *as a result of* — do not count. Distinctive ones do.

The second half of the test matters more and is easier to forget: **is the structure yours?** A passage can share almost no words and still be a copy if it follows the original sentence for sentence, in the same order, foregrounding the same things.

## Citation is separate, and not optional

This trips people up constantly, so it is worth stating flatly.

**Paraphrasing does not remove the need to cite.** Citation credits the *idea*. Rewording changes whose sentence it is, not whose thinking it is. An uncited paraphrase, however skilfully done, presents someone else's analysis as your own — which is precisely what the rules exist to stop.

The only exception is common knowledge in your field, and the boundary is narrower than people assume. If you learned it from the source you are reading, cite it.

There is a grimly ironic failure mode here: a student paraphrases beautifully, produces genuinely original prose, does not cite it, and is penalised more heavily than the one who patchwrote and cited.

## When to quote instead

Paraphrasing is the default, but not always right.

**Quote when the wording is the point.** A definition, a statutory phrase, a line of argument whose phrasing carries the meaning. Restating these loses precision.

**Quote when the claim is contentious.** If you are about to disagree with someone, put their position in their own words first. It is fairer and it protects you from arguing against a version you accidentally softened.

**Quote when you cannot restate it without hedging.** If every paraphrase you attempt feels like it might be misrepresenting them, that is a signal to quote and then discuss.

**Paraphrase everything else.** A page of block quotations demonstrates that you can copy accurately. Marking rewards evidence that you understood.

## Where AI tools sit

Paraphrasing tools do not change either of the two things that decide this.

If the output is uncited, it is plagiarism for the same reason a manual uncited paraphrase is — the idea is still someone else's. And a tool that swaps synonyms while keeping the structure is doing patchwriting for you, automatically, at scale. Faster, and just as detectable.

Separately, many institutions now restrict paraphrasing tools under their AI policies regardless of citation, so a tool that is fine for a blog post may be prohibited for an assignment. Worth being straight about our own tool here: the [Text Humanizer](/humanize-text) works on rhythm and phrasing in text you wrote. It is not a citation tool and it does not make uncited material yours. If the source of a passage is someone else's work, no rewriting tool changes that.

## If you are accused of it

Patchwriting cases are often genuinely accidental, and that is worth saying clearly, because the panic is usually worse than the situation.

Say what your process was. Show your notes and drafts — someone who read, took notes, and wrote from those notes has a trail that looks completely different from someone who worked from an open source document. That evidence is far more persuasive than arguing about how many words overlap, and it is why keeping [a writing audit trail](/blog/keep-a-writing-audit-trail) is worth the small effort before you need it.

If a detector score is also involved, note that similarity and AI indicators are separate measures — [what a Turnitin AI score actually means](/blog/what-a-turnitin-ai-score-means) sets out the difference.

## The version to remember

Read it. Close it. Write it. Check it. Cite it.

If you can explain the idea to someone without looking, you can paraphrase it. If you cannot, no amount of rewording will hide that — and the rewording is where people get caught.
