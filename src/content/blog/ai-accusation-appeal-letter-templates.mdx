Being asked to explain yourself in writing is where most AI accusations are won or lost, and it is the point at which people most often make things worse — usually by writing something furious at eleven at night, or something so apologetic it reads as a confession.

These are four templates covering the situations that actually come up. None should be sent as-is. The specifics are what persuade, and a letter that reads as though it came off the internet undermines the exact impression you are trying to create.

Before writing anything, read [what to do in the first 24 hours](/blog/falsely-accused-of-using-ai) — preserving your version history matters more than any wording, and it has to happen before you reply.

## What every version needs

Four elements, in roughly this order. The order matters as much as the content.

**A direct statement.** Say plainly that you wrote the work. Do not bury it, do not open with the detector's failings. One clear sentence near the top.

**Evidence of process.** What you can show: version history, drafts, notes, sources, timestamps. This is the load-bearing part.

**An offer.** Something that makes resolution easy — a conversation, a supervised writing sample, a walk through your sources.

**The reliability point, last.** The detector's limitations belong at the end, framed as context rather than as attack. Leading with it reads as deflection even when every word is true.

What none of them should contain: outrage, threats to escalate, accusations of incompetence, or a wall of links about detector failures. You want to be the easiest possible person to exonerate.

## Template 1 — First response to an informal query

For the initial email, before anything formal has started. Most cases end here, and a measured reply is why.

> Dear [Name],
>
> Thank you for letting me know about the flag on [assignment name]. I wrote this work myself, and I am glad to show you how.
>
> I drafted it in Google Docs between [date] and [date]. The version history is attached — you can see the introduction rewritten three times, and the section on [topic] was originally the conclusion before I moved it. I have also attached my notes and the annotated sources I used.
>
> I would be glad to discuss the paper in person, including the parts I found difficult and what I cut. If it would help, I am also happy to write a comparable piece under supervision.
>
> My understanding is that AI detection tools estimate how statistically predictable writing is, rather than detecting how it was produced, and that clear, structured prose scores highly for that reason. I would be grateful if the score could be considered alongside the evidence above.
>
> Please let me know what would be most useful.
>
> [Your name] · [Student or staff ID] · [Module or reference]

**Adapting it.** Replace the version-history description with real detail from your own document — a specific paragraph you moved, a section you rewrote. Generic claims about having a version history are far weaker than one concrete example of a change you made. If you did not draft somewhere with version history, replace that paragraph with whatever you do have, and say so directly rather than skipping it.

## Template 2 — Formal appeal

When there has been a finding and you are appealing it. Longer, more structured, and it should engage the actual policy.

> Re: Appeal against academic misconduct finding — [reference number]
>
> Dear [Panel or Officer],
>
> I am appealing the finding of [outcome] in relation to [assignment], dated [date]. I did not use AI to generate this work, and I set out below the evidence and the grounds for appeal.
>
> **Grounds**
>
> 1. The finding rests on an AI detection score without supporting evidence of misconduct.
> 2. [Institution]'s policy at [section] states that [quote the relevant clause].
> 3. Process evidence available at the time was not considered.
>
> **Evidence**
>
> - Full version history for the document, covering [date] to [date], showing composition across [number] editing sessions.
> - Research notes dated [dates], including material I did not use.
> - [Emails, drafts, supervisor correspondence, library records — list what you have.]
>
> **On the detector score**
>
> AI detectors estimate statistical predictability rather than authorship, and published research has found they misclassify writing by [non-native English speakers / in formal registers] at substantially elevated rates. Turnitin's own guidance advises that its AI indicator should not be the sole basis for an academic integrity decision. I am not asking the panel to disregard the score, only to weigh it against the evidence above.
>
> **Requested outcome**
>
> That the finding be set aside and the original submission marked on its merits. I remain willing to discuss the work in detail or complete a supervised writing sample.
>
> [Your name] · [ID] · [Date]

**Adapting it.** The single most important line is the policy quote in ground 2. Find your institution's actual wording — many policies now state explicitly that a detector score alone is insufficient. Quoting their own document is far more effective than any argument you construct. If you cannot find such a clause, delete that ground rather than inventing one.

Keep the requested outcome specific. Panels resolve concrete requests more readily than expressions of unfairness.

## Template 3 — Partial use, disclosed honestly

If you did use AI assistance for part of the work. This is where most real cases sit, and honesty is the only approach that survives scrutiny.

> Dear [Name],
>
> Thank you for raising this. I want to be straightforward about how I worked on this assignment.
>
> The argument, structure, research, and writing are mine. I did use [tool] for [specific use — checking grammar, rephrasing two paragraphs in section 3, suggesting an outline I then rewrote]. I did not use it to generate content, and I have not submitted text I did not write.
>
> I have attached my version history and notes, which show the drafting process and where those edits sit.
>
> I have re-read [Institution]'s policy at [section]. My reading is that this falls within permitted assistance, but if the panel takes a different view I would rather establish that now than have it unclear. I am happy to discuss the work in detail or resubmit without that assistance.
>
> [Your name] · [ID]

**Adapting it.** Precision is the entire point. "I used Grammarly for spelling" and "I asked a model to restructure my argument" are different claims with different consequences, and blurring them is what turns a manageable situation into a serious one.

Do not minimise and do not over-confess. Describe exactly what happened. Every institution treats a discovered cover-up far more harshly than the original conduct — the denial that unravels is what ends up on records, not the assistance.

## Template 4 — Second-language circumstances

If English is not your first language, this is directly relevant evidence and worth stating explicitly.

> Dear [Name],
>
> Thank you for letting me know. I wrote this assignment myself, and I would like to explain why I believe the detection score is high.
>
> English is not my first language. I write carefully and use vocabulary I am confident is correct, which means my word choices tend to be common ones. My understanding is that AI detectors measure how statistically predictable text is, so writing that uses high-frequency vocabulary and consistent sentence structure scores as machine-like regardless of who wrote it.
>
> This is a documented pattern rather than my own theory. Research published in *Patterns* in 2023 found that GPT detectors misclassified essays by non-native English writers at substantially higher rates than essays by native writers, and that the effect disappeared when the same essays were rewritten with more varied vocabulary.
>
> My evidence: version history for the document from [date] to [date], my outline and notes written in [your first language], and [anything else].
>
> I am very willing to discuss the assignment in person or to write a comparable piece under supervision.
>
> [Your name] · [ID]

**Adapting it.** Notes in your first language are close to unforgeable evidence — generated English does not arrive with a Portuguese or Korean outline behind it. If you have any, lead with them.

The research reference is worth keeping. It converts what sounds like an excuse into a documented phenomenon, and it is genuinely peer-reviewed. The mechanism is explained in [why AI detectors fail non-native English speakers](/blog/why-ai-detectors-fail-non-native-speakers).

## Common mistakes

**Sending it the same night.** Write it, sleep, reread in the morning. The version you write while angry is never the one to send.

**Attacking the tool first.** True and tactically wrong. Evidence first, reliability last.

**Over-explaining.** A long letter reads as anxious. Two hundred words of specifics beats a thousand of protest.

**Attaching everything.** Two or three well-chosen exhibits — version history, notes, one source — land better than a folder of forty files nobody opens.

**Asking for sympathy.** Consequences for your visa, your family, or your future are real, and they are not evidence. Mention them separately if there is a genuine mitigation process; keep them out of the factual response.

**Missing the deadline.** Appeal windows are short and enforced. Note the date before you draft anything.

**Rewriting the work.** Do not run it through a rewriting tool after an accusation — including [ours](/humanize-text). It changes the text after the fact, which is indefensible, and if the defended version differs from the submitted one you have created a second problem.

## After you send it

Keep a copy of everything, in a dated folder. Note who you spoke to and when. If the reply is verbal, follow up with a short email summarising what was said — a written record of an informal resolution protects you if it is revisited.

If the stakes include a failed module, a mark on your record, or a visa consequence, get an advocate before the meeting rather than after. Student unions provide trained representatives free, and they have sat through dozens of these. You have sat through none.

And once it is resolved, set up the habits in [keeping a writing audit trail](/blog/keep-a-writing-audit-trail). The evidence that ends these cases has to exist before the accusation does.
