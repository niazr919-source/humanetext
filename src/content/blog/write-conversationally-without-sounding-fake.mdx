"Make it more conversational" is one of the most common pieces of editorial feedback and one of the least useful, because it sends people in two wrong directions at once.

Some writers hear it as *be less careful*, and produce something sloppy. Others hear it as *be friendlier*, and produce something that reads like a brand pretending to be your mate. Both are worse than the stiff draft they started with.

The useful version is narrower: conversational writing means the sentences are shaped the way a competent person shapes them when explaining something out loud. That is a structural property, not a tonal one, and it has almost nothing to do with slang or exclamation marks.

## Two different failures

**Stiff** sounds like this:

> It is important to note that the utilisation of this framework may facilitate improvements in team communication outcomes in certain organisational contexts.

**Performed** sounds like this:

> Okay, so here's the thing — team communication is HARD, right? 😅 But what if I told you there's a way to make it way easier? Let's dive in!

Both are unreadable. The first hides behind formality; the second performs a personality nobody asked for. The failure is the same underneath — in each case the writer is managing an impression rather than saying something.

The version that works:

> This framework helps teams communicate better, though not in every organisation. It works best where people already write things down.

Direct, plain, no performance, and it makes a real claim including a limitation. That is conversational writing.

## The test that settles it

**Could you say this sentence to someone's face without feeling embarrassed?**

Not "would you say it exactly this way" — writing is more compressed than speech, and that is fine. But if reading a sentence aloud to a colleague would make you wince, it is wrong in one direction or the other.

"It is incumbent upon organisations to leverage synergies" fails the test. So does "let's dive in, friends!" Both are things nobody says.

This single question does more work than any list of rules, because it catches both failure modes at once.

## What actually creates the effect

Five structural things, none of which involve informality.

**Contractions.** *It's, don't, you'll, we've.* Their absence is the single strongest signal of stiffness in English, and restoring them changes the feel of a paragraph faster than anything else. Formal writing can use them; most style guides that ban them are older than the reader.

**Direct address.** *You.* Writing about "one" or "the user" or "individuals" puts a pane of glass between writer and reader. Say "you" and the glass goes.

**Short clauses.** Not necessarily short sentences — long sentences are fine if their clauses are short. What makes prose unreadable aloud is stacked subordinate clauses: "the framework, which was developed by a team that had previously worked on projects involving distributed collaboration, offers…" Nobody speaks like that because you cannot hold it in your head.

**Plain word choice.** *Use*, not *utilise*. *Help*, not *facilitate*. *About*, not *regarding*. The formal synonym is almost always the longer, vaguer one, and choosing it is a tell that the writer was performing seriousness.

**Verbs doing the work.** "We decided" rather than "a decision was made." "The system failed" rather than "there was a system failure." Nominalisation — turning verbs into nouns — is the main engine of stiffness in professional writing.

Notice that none of these are jokes, slang, emoji, or enthusiasm. You can apply all five to a legal memo and it will still be a legal memo, only readable.

## Formality and register are different things

The confusion that causes most of the trouble.

**Formality** is how much distance the situation calls for. A condolence letter and a Slack message need different formality, and that is not a matter of skill.

**Register** is whether the writing sounds like a person is behind it. That is available at every level of formality.

A Supreme Court opinion can be direct and forceful while remaining entirely formal. A marketing email can be stiff and evasive while using slang throughout. The two axes are independent, and "be more conversational" almost always means *fix the register*, not *lower the formality*.

This matters because people respond to the feedback by dropping formality — adding jokes to a report, exclamation marks to an announcement — when the actual problem was hedging and nominalisation.

## Where it goes wrong

**Manufactured enthusiasm.** "We're SO excited to announce…" Excitement you did not feel reads as a sales technique, because it is one. Warmth in writing comes from being genuinely useful, not from sounding pleased.

**Rhetorical questions the reader did not ask.** "Ever wondered why some teams just click?" No. Ask a question only when the reader is genuinely holding it.

**Slang you do not use.** Borrowed informality is more obvious than formality. If you would not say "no cap" out loud, do not write it.

**Filler that mimics speech.** "So, basically, here's the thing." Speech is full of filler because we are thinking in real time. Writing is not, and reproducing the filler mimics the surface of conversation while losing what makes it work.

**Over-correcting into fragments.** A fragment used deliberately lands. Fifteen in a row is a tic. Like this. Every time. See?

## Some before-and-afters

> **Stiff:** Prior to the commencement of the project, it is recommended that stakeholders be consulted regarding requirements.
>
> **Conversational:** Talk to the people who'll use it before you start building.

> **Stiff:** The organisation has made the determination that a restructuring of the department is necessary.
>
> **Conversational:** We're restructuring the department. Here's why.

> **Performed:** Ready to supercharge your writing?! Let's gooo 🚀
>
> **Conversational:** Most drafts get better with one change: cut the first paragraph.

The conversational versions are shorter in every case, which is usually true. Stiffness and performance both add words — one to hedge, the other to decorate.

## A pass on a stiff draft

Fifteen minutes, roughly in this order:

1. **Add contractions.** Search for "do not," "it is," "cannot," "you will." Mechanical and immediate.
2. **Replace formal synonyms.** *Utilise, facilitate, commence, regarding, prior to, in order to.* Each has a shorter everyday word.
3. **Turn nominalisations back into verbs.** Search *-tion*, *-ment*, *-ance*.
4. **Switch to "you."** Wherever you wrote "one," "users," or "individuals" and mean the reader.
5. **Break up stacked clauses.** Any sentence you cannot say in one breath.
6. **Cut hedging.** "May potentially," "in certain contexts," "it could be argued."
7. **Read it aloud.** The only check that catches both stiffness and performance.

Step 7 is the one that matters. Everything above is a shortcut to what your ear would have told you anyway.
