"Find your voice" is the least actionable advice in writing. It implies voice is something buried inside you that emerges if you write enough, and that there is nothing to do in the meantime but wait.

That is not really how it works. Voice is the accumulation of choices you make repeatedly — about sentence length, about how much you hedge, about whether you use the first person, about which words you reach for under pressure. Those choices are observable. You can audit them, name them, and decide which to keep.

This matters more than it used to. When a model can produce competent prose about anything, competence stops being a differentiator. What survives is the part that is identifiably yours.

## Voice is not tone

Worth separating, because they get used interchangeably.

**Tone** changes with the situation. You are warmer in a thank-you note than in an incident report. Tone is a dial you move deliberately.

**Voice** is what stays constant across those situations. If your incident report and your thank-you note both favour short declarative sentences, both avoid the passive, and both use a dry aside when things go wrong, that is voice. It persists while tone shifts around it.

The practical consequence: you cannot develop voice by picking a tone. You develop it by noticing what you do regardless of tone, and then doing it on purpose.

## Audit what you already do

Take three or four things you have written that you did not hate — emails count, so do messages to friends, so do internal documents. Ideally at least one where you were writing quickly, because speed strips out affectation and shows habits.

Then measure. Actually measure, not impressionistically.

**Sentence length distribution.** Count the words in every sentence of a few paragraphs. Write down the numbers. You are looking for the spread, not the average. Some writers cluster at 12–18 and rarely leave it. Others swing from 4 to 45. Neither is better; the pattern is information about you.

**Openers.** List the first two words of every sentence on a page. Repetition here is your default move — "The," "It," "But," "This means." Everyone has two or three they lean on.

**Paragraph length.** Do you write in even blocks or ragged ones? Do you ever give a sentence its own paragraph?

**Punctuation habits.** Count em dashes, semicolons, parentheses, colons. Most people have one connector they overuse. Mine is the em dash. Knowing that is what lets me cut half of them in editing rather than not seeing them at all.

**Person and distance.** Do you write "I," "we," "you," or none? Do you make claims directly or attribute them ("research suggests," "it seems")?

**Concreteness.** Pick a paragraph and mark every noun as either concrete (a thing you could photograph) or abstract. The ratio is one of the strongest determinants of whether writing feels alive, and it is highly trainable.

**Humour.** Where does it appear — never, in asides, in the structure of arguments? Dry or warm?

Twenty minutes of this tells you more about your voice than a year of being told to find it.

## Name three properties and commit

From the audit, pick three characteristics to make deliberate. Three, not ten — a style sheet you cannot remember is a style sheet you will not use.

Make them specific enough to check. "Conversational" is not checkable. These are:

- *Short sentences carry the important claims. Long ones do the explaining.*
- *No hedging on things I actually know. If I am unsure, say why, not "arguably."*
- *One concrete example per section, minimum, drawn from something that actually happened.*

Now you have a test you can run on any draft. That is the whole trick — converting a feeling into a checklist.

## Steal deliberately, in pieces

Every writer develops by imitation. The failure mode is imitating a whole writer, which produces a bad copy of them and no you.

Take single moves instead. From one writer, the habit of opening with a specific scene rather than a general claim. From another, the willingness to end a section on an unresolved question. From a third, technical precision without jargon.

Practise one at a time, on your own material. Write a paragraph you would normally open generally, and open it with a scene instead. Do it enough times and it stops being an imitation and becomes an option available to you, which is the point.

Assembled from enough sources, the borrowings stop being traceable. That is not a compromise — it is how every voice is built.

## The habits that suppress voice

More often than not, voice is not missing. It is being flattened by habits picked up from institutional writing.

**Hedging.** "It could be argued that," "somewhat," "arguably," "in many cases." Each one puts distance between you and your claim. A page with fifteen of them has no author in it. Academic and corporate writing train this deeply, because unhedged claims can be challenged and hedged ones cannot.

**The passive voice as a shield.** "Mistakes were made" is the joke version, but "the decision was taken to postpone" does the same work. The passive has legitimate uses — when the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant — and a much larger illegitimate one, which is avoiding saying who did it.

**Abstraction.** "Stakeholder alignment challenges" instead of "the finance team and the product team wanted different things." The abstract version is shorter and says nothing. Concrete nouns are where voice lives, because choosing *which* concrete detail is a judgement only you make.

**Nominalisation.** Turning verbs into nouns — "the implementation of" rather than "we implemented." It drains energy and hides the actor at the same time.

**Borrowed register.** Writing in the voice of the last document you read, whether that is a legal contract or a LinkedIn post. This one is worth watching for specifically after you have been reading a lot of anything.

Removing these does not add voice. It stops removing it, which is most of the battle.

## Reading aloud is the whole practice

The single most effective technique, and the most reliably ignored.

Read a draft aloud, at normal speaking pace, without correcting as you go. Mark where you stumble, where you run out of breath, where you get bored, and where you naturally rephrase — that last one especially. If your mouth spontaneously produces a better version than what is on the page, use the version your mouth produced.

This works because speech carries rhythm and register that silent reading smooths over. It catches uniform sentence length, unpronounceable clause pileups, and register mismatches, all at once, without your having to know the terminology for any of them.

Text-to-speech is a decent substitute and has one advantage: it will not unconsciously perform the emphasis you intended, so it exposes places where the emphasis is not actually on the page.

## Keeping your voice when you use AI

This is where the audit earns its keep, because generic drafting pulls hard toward the statistical middle.

**Never let a model draft cold.** A model given nothing produces the average voice. Give it your own material — an outline in your words, a paragraph you have already written, a voice sample of a few hundred words from the audit. Our guide to [briefing an AI](/blog/how-to-brief-an-ai-for-a-usable-draft) goes into the mechanics.

**Write the parts that are yours yourself.** The opening, the specific example, the sentence that carries the argument, the ending. Let the model handle transitions and the sections that are genuinely routine. A piece where the load-bearing sentences are yours reads as yours even if half the connective tissue was drafted.

**Run your three-property checklist on every draft.** Does it hedge where you would not? Is there a concrete example per section? Are the important claims in short sentences? This takes two minutes and catches most of the drift.

**Reintroduce your punctuation.** Models have their own habits — heavy on the em dash and the tricolon, light on the semicolon and the parenthesis. Your distribution is different. Restoring it does more for recognisability than most people expect.

**Read the finished thing aloud.** If it does not sound like you, it is not finished. This is the check that catches everything the others miss.

Our [Text Humanizer](/humanize-text) varies rhythm and phrasing while holding meaning fixed, which helps with the flatness. What it cannot do is supply your judgement about which detail matters or which claim to make without hedging. That is the part that is actually you, and it is the part worth protecting.

## It takes less time than people think

The audit is an afternoon. The three properties take a week of deliberate use before they start happening automatically. Reading aloud never stops being necessary but stops feeling like an imposition after a month.

What takes longer is the accumulation — the point where your choices are consistent enough that a reader could identify a paragraph as yours without a byline. That is years. But you do not need to get there for the work to pay off. A draft that is unhedged, concrete, and rhythmically varied is already better than most of what is published, and every one of those three is a decision rather than a gift.
