"Free" is doing a lot of work in this category. Nearly every tool has a free tier somewhere on its pricing page, and a large fraction of them are free in the sense that a test drive is free.

That is not a scandal — lead generation is a legitimate business model, and someone has to pay for the inference. The problem is that the terms are usually discoverable only after you have invested time, pasted in your text, and hit a wall. This is a guide to reading the terms first.

## The five places limits hide

Free tiers differ along five axes, and marketing copy rarely mentions more than one of them.

### 1. Whether an account is required at all

The first fork. Some tools process text immediately; others require an email, a password, and sometimes email verification before you can rewrite a single sentence.

An account requirement is not automatically bad — it is how a tool offers a persistent history or higher limits. But there is a meaningful difference between "sign up to get more" and "sign up to get anything." The second is a trial gated behind a lead capture, and it means you cannot evaluate the output quality before deciding whether the product is worth your address.

A useful test: open the tool in a private browsing window and see how far you get. If you cannot see a single rewritten sentence without registering, the free tier is a demo.

### 2. Whether the allowance renews

The difference between a **recurring** allowance and a **one-time** one is the single largest variable, and the one most often obscured.

"1,000 free words" could mean a thousand words a day, forever, or a thousand words total across the lifetime of your account. Both get written the same way on a pricing page. The first is a usable free tool; the second is a sample.

Look for the words "per day," "per month," or "trial." If none of them appear, assume it is one-time and check the FAQ.

### 3. Whether the free output is the same output

Some tools run a smaller or older model on the free tier, or cap the depth of rewriting. Others apply the same processing and only limit volume.

This one matters more than it sounds, because it breaks your ability to evaluate. If free output is deliberately weaker, you cannot tell whether the paid tier is genuinely better or merely unthrottled — and you are being asked to pay to find out.

Tools that do this rarely advertise it. The tell is usually vague pricing-page language: "enhanced quality," "advanced rewriting," "premium engine," without saying what is enhanced relative to what.

### 4. What the input caps are

Two separate limits are often in play: how much text per request, and how much per period. A 300-word-per-request cap makes a tool unusable for anything longer than an email regardless of how generous the daily total is, because you will be splitting your document by hand and losing coherence across the joins.

Check the per-request cap specifically. It is frequently buried in the interface rather than on the pricing page.

### 5. What happens to your text

The one almost nobody checks. Free tiers are sometimes subsidised by the data. Read the privacy policy for two things: whether submitted text is retained, and whether it may be used to train models.

For a paragraph of blog copy this may not concern you. For anything confidential — client work, unpublished research, internal documents, anything under NDA — it should be the first thing you check, before pricing. If a policy does not address retention at all, treat that as a no.

## A checklist before you paste anything

1. Does it produce output in a private window with no account?
2. Does the allowance say "per day" or "per month," or is it a one-time total?
3. Is the free-tier output the same processing as paid, or a reduced version?
4. What is the per-request word cap, not just the period cap?
5. Does the privacy policy state whether text is retained or used for training?
6. If you give an email, what else does it get used for? (Check for a marketing opt-in that is pre-ticked.)

Two minutes of this will tell you more than any review, including this one.

## Why so many free tiers are structured this way

Worth understanding, because it explains the pattern rather than just cataloguing it.

Every rewrite costs the provider real money in inference. A tool serving unlimited free rewrites at any scale is losing money on every request, so free tiers exist to convert. The design question for the operator is where to put the wall: early enough that conversion happens, late enough that people can tell the product works.

Tools that put the wall before any output are optimising hard for conversion, and usually reflect a business under pressure to show signup numbers. Tools that let you use them properly and cap the volume are betting that a good experience converts better than a forced one. Neither is dishonest. But the first design tells you the free tier was never meant to be used, and you should not plan around it.

The exception is tools where the processing is genuinely cheap. Image effects that run on open-source libraries, or rewriting handled by a small local model, cost the operator very little per use — those can afford genuinely open access in a way that anything calling a large frontier model cannot.

## When free is the wrong question

If you are rewriting a paragraph occasionally, free tiers across this category are fine and the differences are mostly friction.

If you are processing volume — a content operation, a translation workflow, a team — free tiers are the wrong frame entirely. What matters then is per-word cost at your volume, whether there is an API, whether output is consistent enough to build a process around, and what the data handling terms actually say. Several tools that look expensive on the pricing page are cheaper at volume than stitching together free tiers, and considerably cheaper than the staff time spent working around limits.

And for the largest category of user — someone with a draft that reads stiffly and no intention of buying anything — the honest answer is that a fair amount of this is doable by hand. Reading the draft aloud, cutting hedging phrases, and varying sentence length gets most of the way there for free, permanently, with no account. A tool is faster; it is not the only route.
