Most schools and workplaces now have some policy on AI-assisted writing, and most of those policies are more nuanced than "banned" or "allowed." The honest answer to "can I use AI to help write this" is almost always "it depends what you mean by help" — so here's a framework that actually holds up.

## The distinction that matters: assistance vs. substitution

**Assistance** is using AI the way you'd use a thesaurus, a grammar checker, or a study partner who reads your draft and points out where it's unclear — a tool that helps you produce work that's still substantively yours. Brainstorming an outline, checking whether a paragraph makes sense, getting unstuck on a first sentence, or rewriting your own already-written draft for clarity are all assistance.

**Substitution** is having AI produce work and submitting it as though you wrote it entirely yourself, with no meaningful contribution beyond a prompt. This is the version that violates most academic integrity policies and a growing number of workplace expectations — not because AI was involved at all, but because the work isn't actually yours.

The line isn't about which tool you used. It's about whether you could explain, defend, and stand behind every claim and idea in the final piece.

## What most institutions actually check for

Academic integrity policies increasingly focus less on "was AI used" (nearly impossible to enforce reliably — see our piece on [how AI detectors work and why they're unreliable](/blog/how-ai-content-detectors-work)) and more on:

- **Disclosure requirements** — many programs now ask you to note where and how you used AI tools, similar to citing a source
- **Understanding checks** — being able to explain or defend what you submitted, in your own words, on request
- **Originality of ideas** — whether the actual arguments and analysis are yours, regardless of what tool helped you phrase them

## A practical checklist before you submit anything

1. **Did you generate the ideas, or did the AI?** If AI produced the argument and you just formatted it, that's substitution, not assistance.
2. **Do you understand every claim well enough to explain it without the AI's help?** If not, you're not ready to submit it.
3. **Does your institution or employer require disclosure?** If yes, disclose — this is usually the actual policy violation people run into, not AI use itself.
4. **Would you be comfortable if someone asked how you wrote this?** If the honest answer would be awkward, that's the real signal something's off — not the tool.

## Where a tool like Humanetext fits into this — and where it doesn't

The [Text Humanizer](/humanize-text) is built to rewrite phrasing and rhythm in text you provide — it doesn't generate new ideas, arguments, or claims. If you write your own draft and use it to fix robotic-sounding phrasing, that's editing, the same category as running spell-check or asking a friend to read it over. If you ask an AI to write an essay from scratch and then run it through any humanizing tool to make it read more naturally, the underlying issue is the same as submitting AI-generated work unedited: the ideas aren't yours, and no amount of rewriting phrasing changes that.

This is also why our own positioning is about **content authenticity**, not detector evasion — read more on that distinction in [our piece on content authenticity in the AI era](/blog/content-authenticity-in-the-ai-era). A tool that helps your own writing sound more like you isn't solving the same problem as a tool built to help you submit someone else's (or something else's) work as your own.

## The short version

Use AI to get unstuck, to check your work, or to fix phrasing in something you actually wrote — that's assistance, and it's increasingly normal and accepted. Don't use it to skip the part where you actually think through and write the thing — that's substitution, and it's the part every serious policy is actually trying to catch.

## Read your actual policy

Almost everything above is general reasoning. The thing that will determine what happens to you is a specific document, and most people accused of misconduct have never opened it.

Policies vary far more than people assume. Broadly, four positions exist:

**Prohibition.** No generative AI in assessed work at all. Increasingly rare and usually confined to specific assessment types.

**Permitted with disclosure.** Use is allowed if declared, often with a required statement describing what the tool did. This is becoming the most common position.

**Permitted for defined purposes.** Language support, grammar, and brainstorming allowed; generation of content or argument prohibited. This tier explicitly protects the second-language use case.

**Module-level discretion.** The institution sets a framework and each convenor decides. This produces the most confusion, because rules differ between two modules in the same term.

Three things to check specifically. First, whether the policy covers *editing* tools or only *generative* ones — many predate the distinction and are ambiguous about grammar checkers. Second, whether disclosure is required and in what form. Third, whether a detector score alone can support a finding; many policies now say explicitly that it cannot.

If your policy is genuinely ambiguous about a tool you want to use, ask in writing before you submit and keep the reply. An email saying "yes, that's fine" from your convenor is worth more than any argument afterwards.

## The second-language complication

There is a group for whom this is considerably harder, and it deserves stating plainly.

Students writing in a second language have always used language support — dictionaries, writing centres, proofreaders, Grammarly. That support has never been controversial and is often explicitly provided by the institution. AI tools do the same job more effectively, which is why uptake among this group is high, and entirely reasonable.

The complication is that these same students are disproportionately flagged by detectors, for reasons that have nothing to do with tool use. Detectors measure vocabulary predictability, and second-language writing is predictable because a smaller active vocabulary means more common word choices. The mechanism is set out in [why AI detectors fail non-native English speakers](/blog/why-ai-detectors-fail-non-native-speakers), and the research finding is stark: essays by non-native writers get misclassified at dramatically higher rates.

So this group faces elevated risk of accusation *and* the strongest legitimate reason to use the tools. If that is you: check whether your policy permits language support explicitly, use the permitted tools without embarrassment, keep your drafting history, and know that the detector's opinion of your writing says more about your vocabulary range than about your honesty.

## Grey areas, with actual answers

The four questions earlier resolve clear cases. These are the ones that come up in practice.

**Grammar and spelling correction.** Fine everywhere. It has been part of writing for decades and no serious policy targets it.

**Rewriting a paragraph you wrote so it reads better.** Almost always fine — it is editing, the category that includes writing centres and asking a friend. Check whether your policy distinguishes editing from generation, since some do not.

**Asking for an outline before you write.** Grey. The structure of an argument is often part of what is being assessed. If the assessment is about constructing an argument, having the structure supplied is closer to substitution than it looks.

**Asking for explanation of a concept you then write about yourself.** Fine, and genuinely useful. This is what a textbook or a tutor does. You still have to understand it well enough to write it.

**Having it summarise a source you have not read.** Not fine, for a reason that has nothing to do with AI policy: you cannot verify the summary, and models misrepresent sources. If you cite it, you are vouching for something you have not checked.

**Asking it to check your argument for weaknesses.** Fine. This is what a peer reviewer does, and the improvements are still yours to make.

**Generating text you then rewrite substantially.** The hard case. Ask the second question honestly — could you explain every claim without help? If the ideas came from the model and you improved the prose, that is substitution with extra steps regardless of how much you rewrote.

**Translating your own writing from your first language.** Usually fine and often explicitly permitted, but check. Some policies treat translation as language support, others as generation.

## If you are accused

Institutions do act on detector scores despite the reliability problems, and false accusations happen to people who did nothing wrong.

The short version: preserve your version history before you touch anything else, find the policy and its appeal deadline, offer to discuss the work in detail in person, and be exactly honest about any assistance you did use. Partial use disclosed openly is defensible; a denial that later unravels is treated far more seriously than the original conduct.

The full sequence — what to preserve, how to word a response, when to bring an advocate — is in [falsely accused of using AI: a practical guide to defending your work](/blog/falsely-accused-of-using-ai).

## Why the line is where it is

It is tempting to treat all of this as arbitrary institutional anxiety. It is worth remembering what assessment is for.

A degree certifies that you can do something. The essay is not the product; it is evidence about you. When the evidence is manufactured, the certification stops meaning anything — and it stops meaning anything for everyone who holds the same qualification, including you, later, when you need it to be worth something.

That is why the line falls between assistance and substitution rather than between tool use and no tool use. Assistance leaves the evidence intact. Substitution replaces it with evidence about a model. Nothing about that reasoning depends on the technology, which is why it will survive the next tool as well.
