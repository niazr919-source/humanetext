Most schools and workplaces now have some policy on AI-assisted writing, and most of those policies are more nuanced than "banned" or "allowed." The honest answer to "can I use AI to help write this" is almost always "it depends what you mean by help" — so here's a framework that actually holds up.

## The distinction that matters: assistance vs. substitution

**Assistance** is using AI the way you'd use a thesaurus, a grammar checker, or a study partner who reads your draft and points out where it's unclear — a tool that helps you produce work that's still substantively yours. Brainstorming an outline, checking whether a paragraph makes sense, getting unstuck on a first sentence, or rewriting your own already-written draft for clarity are all assistance.

**Substitution** is having AI produce work and submitting it as though you wrote it entirely yourself, with no meaningful contribution beyond a prompt. This is the version that violates most academic integrity policies and a growing number of workplace expectations — not because AI was involved at all, but because the work isn't actually yours.

The line isn't about which tool you used. It's about whether you could explain, defend, and stand behind every claim and idea in the final piece.

## What most institutions actually check for

Academic integrity policies increasingly focus less on "was AI used" (nearly impossible to enforce reliably — see our piece on [how AI detectors work and why they're unreliable](/blog/how-ai-content-detectors-work)) and more on:

- **Disclosure requirements** — many programs now ask you to note where and how you used AI tools, similar to citing a source
- **Understanding checks** — being able to explain or defend what you submitted, in your own words, on request
- **Originality of ideas** — whether the actual arguments and analysis are yours, regardless of what tool helped you phrase them

## A practical checklist before you submit anything

1. **Did you generate the ideas, or did the AI?** If AI produced the argument and you just formatted it, that's substitution, not assistance.
2. **Do you understand every claim well enough to explain it without the AI's help?** If not, you're not ready to submit it.
3. **Does your institution or employer require disclosure?** If yes, disclose — this is usually the actual policy violation people run into, not AI use itself.
4. **Would you be comfortable if someone asked how you wrote this?** If the honest answer would be awkward, that's the real signal something's off — not the tool.

## Where a tool like Humanwords fits into this — and where it doesn't

The [Text Humanizer](/humanize-text) is built to rewrite phrasing and rhythm in text you provide — it doesn't generate new ideas, arguments, or claims. If you write your own draft and use it to fix robotic-sounding phrasing, that's editing, the same category as running spell-check or asking a friend to read it over. If you ask an AI to write an essay from scratch and then run it through any humanizing tool to make it read more naturally, the underlying issue is the same as submitting AI-generated work unedited: the ideas aren't yours, and no amount of rewriting phrasing changes that.

This is also why our own positioning is about **content authenticity**, not detector evasion — read more on that distinction in [our piece on content authenticity in the AI era](/blog/content-authenticity-in-the-ai-era). A tool that helps your own writing sound more like you isn't solving the same problem as a tool built to help you submit someone else's (or something else's) work as your own.

## The short version

Use AI to get unstuck, to check your work, or to fix phrasing in something you actually wrote — that's assistance, and it's increasingly normal and accepted. Don't use it to skip the part where you actually think through and write the thing — that's substitution, and it's the part every serious policy is actually trying to catch.
