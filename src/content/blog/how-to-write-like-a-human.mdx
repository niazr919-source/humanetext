"Write like a human" is strange advice to need. You are one.

But the request makes sense, because most of us learned to write in places that trained it out of us. School rewarded the five-paragraph essay. University rewarded hedging, because an unhedged claim can be marked wrong. Work rewarded the passive voice, because "mistakes were made" is safer than naming who made them. Each of those environments taught a habit that survives long after the environment does.

So this is not really a guide to acquiring something. It is a guide to removing what was installed, and then making a handful of deliberate choices in its place. Everything below is specific enough to check on a draft you already have.

## What "human" actually means

Not informality. Plenty of formal writing reads as unmistakably human, and plenty of chatty writing reads as machine-made.

The distinguishing quality is that a person made **decisions with something at stake**. They committed to a claim they could be wrong about. They chose one example over another because it was the one they remembered. They gave four paragraphs to the interesting part and one line to the routine part, because they had a view about which was which.

Writing that sounds inhuman is usually writing where nobody decided anything — every claim hedged, every section the same length, every example generic enough to be unobjectionable. That happens when a model produces text, and it happens just as reliably when a committee does.

Which means the fix is the same in both cases.

## 1. Vary your sentence lengths

The most mechanical fix, and the one with the largest immediate effect.

Take any paragraph and count the words in each sentence. If the numbers cluster — 19, 21, 18, 22 — the paragraph will read as flat no matter how good the individual sentences are. A paragraph that runs 6, 24, 11, 4, 31 has rhythm, and rhythm is most of what people mean by "flow."

The reason is emphasis. **The shortest sentence in a paragraph is the one readers remember**, because length creates contrast. So where you put your short sentence determines what the paragraph appears to be about.

A rough target for general prose: most sentences between eight and twenty-five words, at least one under eight per paragraph or two, and the occasional long one running past thirty when an idea genuinely needs the room. Do not write to those numbers deliberately — that produces its own artificial evenness. Use them to diagnose when something feels off and you cannot say why.

The full mechanics are in [sentence rhythm](/blog/sentence-rhythm-writing-tips).

## 2. Be specific

The single highest-return change available, and the one no tool can make for you.

> Effective communication is essential for organisational success.

> Our engineering team shipped on time for four quarters. Design missed every deadline in the same period. The difference was that engineering wrote decisions down and design discussed them in meetings nobody minuted.

The second is longer, and it says something. The first could appear in any document about any organisation, which is another way of saying it says nothing.

Specificity is what makes writing feel like it came from someone who was actually there. It is also the thing generated text is structurally worst at, because a model has no experience to draw on — only the average of everything written on a subject.

Practically: **one concrete thing per section, minimum.** A number, a name, a date, an incident, something that broke. If you cannot supply one, that is worth noticing — it usually means you do not know the subject as well as the draft implies.

## 3. Say what you think

Hedging is the most common thing that makes competent writing feel lifeless.

> It could be argued that regular maintenance may potentially help to extend equipment lifespan in many cases.

> Regular maintenance roughly doubles how long equipment lasts.

Every hedge in the first version puts distance between the writer and the claim. Stack fifteen of them across a page and there is no author left in the text.

Hedges have a legitimate use: marking genuine uncertainty. "The evidence is mixed" is useful when the evidence is mixed. What kills prose is decorative hedging — softening things you actually know, because an unqualified statement can be challenged.

The test: for each hedge, ask whether you are genuinely unsure. If you are, keep it and say *why* you are unsure, which is more informative than the hedge alone. If you are not, delete it.

## 4. Distribute attention unevenly

Look at a draft from ten feet away. If every section is roughly the same length and every paragraph is three or four sentences, it will read as a form being filled in — because that is structurally what it is.

Real writing is lumpy. The part the writer found most interesting gets disproportionate space. A section where there was only one thing to say is allowed to be four sentences long. A paragraph that needs to land hard gets to be one sentence.

If every section of your draft is 300 words, the outline drove the writing rather than the material. That is worth fixing at the structural level, before any sentence-polishing.

## 5. Cut the institutional habits

Four patterns, all learned, all removable. Each one exists to protect the writer from being pinned down, and each one costs you a reader.

**The passive voice used as a shield.** "The decision was taken to postpone" hides who decided. The passive is fine when the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant — "the samples were refrigerated overnight" is correct. It is a problem when it is doing concealment work. [When each voice is right](/blog/active-and-passive-voice) covers the distinction.

**Nominalisation.** Verbs turned into nouns, propped up by a weak verb. "The implementation of the new system resulted in an improvement in efficiency" instead of "the new system made us more efficient." Search your draft for *-tion*, *-ment*, and *-ance* endings; each is a candidate for turning back into a verb.

**Abstraction.** "Stakeholder alignment challenges" instead of "the finance team and the product team wanted different things." The abstract version is shorter and communicates less.

**Borrowed register.** Writing in the voice of the last document you read. Worth watching for after a stretch of reading anything with a strong house style.

## 6. Read it aloud

The single most effective technique in this article, and the most reliably skipped.

Read your draft aloud at normal speaking pace without correcting as you go. Mark where you stumble, where you run out of breath, where you get bored, and — most usefully — **where your mouth spontaneously produces a better sentence than the one on the page.** Use that version.

This works because speech carries rhythm and register that silent reading smooths over. It catches uniform sentence length, unpronounceable clause pileups, and register mismatches at once, without your needing to know the terminology for any of them.

Text-to-speech is a reasonable substitute with one advantage: it will not unconsciously perform the emphasis you intended, so it exposes places where the emphasis is not actually on the page.

## 7. Develop a voice on purpose

"Find your voice" implies waiting. In practice voice is an accumulation of choices you make repeatedly, and you can audit them.

Take three things you have written that you did not hate. Measure: your sentence-length spread, your habitual sentence openers, your punctuation tics, whether you write "I" or "we" or neither, and your ratio of concrete to abstract nouns.

Then pick **three** characteristics to make deliberate. Three, not ten — a style sheet you cannot remember is one you will not use. Make them checkable:

- *Short sentences carry the important claims. Long ones do the explaining.*
- *No hedging on things I actually know.*
- *One concrete example per section, from something that happened.*

Now you have a test you can run on any draft. That is the whole trick: converting a feeling into a checklist. The longer method is in [finding your writing voice](/blog/how-to-find-your-writing-voice).

## The order to work in

Doing these in the wrong order wastes effort, because early passes change what the later ones operate on.

1. **Check the facts.** No point polishing a sentence containing a number you cannot source.
2. **Find the claim.** State the piece's point in one sentence. If you cannot, the problem is structural.
3. **Cut.** Filler openers, restated sentences, summary conclusions. Deletion solves more than rewriting, and it makes what remains easier to see. [How to cut a draft](/blog/how-to-cut-your-draft) has the order.
4. **Add specifics.** One concrete thing per section.
5. **Unhedge** what you actually know.
6. **Fix the rhythm.** Sentence lengths, repeated openers, paragraph shape.
7. **Read it aloud.**

Most advice starts at step 6. Steps 1 to 5 are where drafts actually improve.

Our [Text Humanizer](/humanize-text) does step 6 — it varies rhythm, breaks up repeated openers, and cuts hedging filler while holding your meaning fixed. That is genuinely useful when a draft reads flat and you are short of time.

Which is the honest summary of this whole guide: the mechanical layer can be assisted, and the layer that makes writing worth reading cannot.
