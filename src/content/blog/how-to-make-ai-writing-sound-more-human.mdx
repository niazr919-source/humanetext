If you've ever pasted AI-generated text into a document and felt something was slightly *off* — even though every sentence was grammatically correct — you've run into the biggest problem with AI writing: it's fluent, but it doesn't sound like a person wrote it.

That gap between "correct" and "natural" is what this guide is about. Here's exactly what to look for and how to fix it.

## Why AI writing feels different in the first place

Large language models are trained to produce the statistically likely next word, which means they gravitate toward the safest, most average phrasing available. The result is text that's technically fine but rhythmically flat — every sentence is a similar length, the same transition words show up again and again, and ideas are often stated rather than shown.

Human writing, by contrast, is uneven on purpose. A writer will drop in a three-word sentence for emphasis, then follow it with a longer one that winds through a qualification or an aside. That unevenness isn't a flaw — it's a signal that someone was actually thinking as they wrote, not filling in a template.

## Start with sentence rhythm

This is the single highest-leverage fix. Read your draft out loud, or run it through a text-to-speech tool, and listen for a metronome effect — sentence after sentence landing at roughly the same length and cadence.

Fix it by:

- Breaking one long sentence into a short one and a longer one
- Combining two short, choppy sentences into a single one with a comma or dash
- Starting a sentence with a conjunction ("But," "And," "So") occasionally, the way people actually talk
- Letting a sentence run a beat longer than feels "safe," then cutting it short

You don't need a formula here. You just need variation, and you'll notice it immediately once you start reading for rhythm instead of correctness.

## Cut the hedging and filler

AI models tend to reach for a specific set of phrases that add length without adding meaning: *"It's important to note that,"* *"In today's fast-paced world,"* *"When it comes to,"* *"At the end of the day."* None of these are wrong, exactly — they're just empty calories. A human writer under a deadline doesn't reach for them; someone padding a word count does.

Go through your draft and delete every sentence that could disappear without losing information. If a sentence exists only to introduce the next sentence, it's probably filler.

## Replace generic claims with specifics

"Effective communication is essential for organizational success" is a sentence that could describe literally any company on Earth. It's true, but it says nothing. Human writing tends to reach for something concrete: a number, an example, a specific scenario.

Compare:

> Generic: "Many businesses struggle with customer retention."

> Specific: "A SaaS company losing 5% of customers a month needs to replace nearly half its user base every year just to stand still."

The second version does more work in fewer words, because it gives the reader something to picture.

## Watch your paragraph structure

AI-generated content has a tell in its structure, not just its sentences: intro paragraph, three or four body paragraphs that each open with a topic sentence and close with a summary, then a closing paragraph that restates the introduction. It's the five-paragraph essay format, and while it's a fine skeleton for a first draft, it reads as templated when it's this consistent across an entire piece.

Break the pattern. Let one paragraph be two sentences. Let another run long. Drop a list where a paragraph would have gone. Real writing doesn't move in even, predictable blocks.

## Read it as if you were the reader, not the writer

The fastest way to catch robotic phrasing is to stop reading your own draft as the person who wrote it (or prompted it) and start reading it as someone encountering it cold. Would you actually say this sentence out loud to a colleague? If not, rewrite it the way you'd actually say it.
