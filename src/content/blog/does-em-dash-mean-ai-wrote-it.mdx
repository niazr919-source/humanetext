Somewhere in 2025, a punctuation mark became evidence.

Post something with an em dash in it and you can reasonably expect a reply asking whether you wrote it yourself. Writers have started stripping them out of finished drafts, and some report training themselves out of a habit they had held for decades.

The observation underneath this is real. The conclusion drawn from it is not.

## What is actually true

Language models do use em dashes more than the average writer. That part holds up.

But the more precise version is that they overuse **one construction**, not the mark in general. The recurring shape is the parenthetical appositive: a dash, a qualifying phrase, another dash, dropped into the middle of a sentence.

> The approach — which had been tested extensively — produced consistent results.

> Her argument — carefully constructed and difficult to dismiss — changed the discussion.

Models reach for this constantly, and there is a mechanical reason. It is a safe way to add nuance without committing to a second sentence. It softens a claim while keeping the sentence moving. Hedging without cost, which is exactly what a system optimised for inoffensive output would converge on.

Human writers use em dashes too, but for a wider range of jobs: an abrupt turn, a punchline, a trailing thought, a summary snapped onto the end of a clause. And crucially, we use them **irregularly**. Three in one paragraph, then none for two pages.

The tell was never the mark. It was the regularity.

## Why the rule spread anyway

Because "AI uses em dashes" fits in a tweet and "AI overproduces one specific appositive construction at unusually even density" does not.

This is how most AI-detection folk wisdom forms. Someone notices a genuine pattern, compresses it into a rule simple enough to repeat, and the compression loses the part that made it true. The same thing happened to the word "delve", to the phrase "it is important to note", and to the rule of three.

Each of those started as a real observation about frequency. Each became a claim about presence. And presence proves nothing — every one of those words and constructions has been in ordinary use for centuries.

We catalogue the broader set of these in [15 words and phrases that give away AI writing](/blog/15-words-phrases-that-give-away-ai-writing) and, at the structural level, in [12 sentence patterns that make prose sound machine-made](/blog/sentence-patterns-that-sound-machine-made). The framing in both is deliberate: density, not presence.

## The damage the rule does

Here is what makes this more than a pedantic quibble.

People are now writing worse on purpose. They are removing a mark they used correctly, replacing it with punctuation that fits less well, because they are afraid of being accused. That is a real loss, and it buys nothing — no detector reports an em dash as its reason, and no reader who was going to suspect you will be reassured by its absence.

It is the same trade we described in [does Grammarly get flagged as AI](/blog/does-grammarly-get-flagged-as-ai): degrading your writing to satisfy a broken measurement. The measurement does not get better. Your prose gets worse.

There is also a fairness problem. The writers most affected are the ones with the most developed punctuation habits — people who learned to use the dash deliberately, often decades ago. Being told that a mark you use well is now suspicious is a peculiar kind of penalty for competence, and it rhymes with what happens to [non-native English speakers under AI detectors](/blog/why-ai-detectors-fail-non-native-speakers), who get flagged for writing carefully.

## What actually reads as mechanical

If you want your punctuation to read as human, the fix is not subtraction. It is variation.

Take a piece of your own writing and find every mid-sentence interruption. Look at how each is punctuated. If they are all dashes, that sameness is the thing a reader picks up on, and it would be equally noticeable if they were all brackets or all commas.

The four ways to punctuate an interruption, and roughly what each does:

**Em dashes** create the sharpest break. The interruption feels like a genuine cut across the sentence, which makes them good for emphasis and bad if used constantly.

**Commas** are the quietest. The interruption stays inside the flow of the sentence, which suits information the reader can absorb without stopping.

**Brackets** signal that the material is genuinely optional — an aside the sentence would survive without.

**A colon or a full stop** is the option people forget. Often the cleanest fix for a dash-heavy sentence is to end it and start another.

Mix these according to what each interruption is doing, and the uniformity disappears without you giving up anything. The wider principle — that variation is the strongest signal of a person writing — is the subject of [sentence rhythm](/blog/sentence-rhythm-writing-tips).

## The three marks, since they get confused

Worth settling, because misuse here is far more common than overuse.

A **hyphen** (-) joins words: *well-written*, *twenty-three*, *state-of-the-art*.

An **en dash** (–) spans a range: *pages 10–24*, *the 1914–18 war*.

An **em dash** (—) separates a phrase from the sentence around it, the job everything above is about.

If you are worried about looking careless, using a hyphen where an em dash belongs is the error a reader will actually notice.

## If someone accuses you

Do not argue about punctuation. It is not a winnable conversation and it makes the mark the subject rather than your work.

Show process instead. Version history, earlier drafts, notes — the same evidence that settles every other version of this. A document that visibly grew over several sessions answers the question in a way no argument about typography can. The full method is in [how to keep a writing audit trail](/blog/keep-a-writing-audit-trail), and if the accusation is formal, [falsely accused of using AI](/blog/falsely-accused-of-using-ai) covers the sequence.

## The part worth keeping

The em dash is not a confession. It is a mark that has been in continuous use since at least the eighteenth century, favoured by writers with far better ears than any current model.

What changed is not the punctuation. It is that a generation of text arrived using one of its constructions at a rate no human ever did, and readers learned the shape of it. That shape is worth knowing — it is genuinely useful for reading critically.

But it is a shape, not a mark. Keep your dashes. Vary what you do with them.
