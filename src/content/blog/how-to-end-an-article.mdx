Most drafts end twice. There is the real ending — the last paragraph that says something — and then there is a summary paragraph after it, recapping what the reader just read.

That second one is almost always deletable, and deleting it usually improves the piece. It occupies the position of maximum emphasis, which is the end, and spends it on repetition.

This is one of the most durable habits from school essay instruction, and it is worth unlearning deliberately.

## Why the summary conclusion persists

Three reasons, none of which apply to writing people read voluntarily.

**Essay format taught it.** Tell them what you'll say, say it, tell them what you said. That structure exists so an examiner can verify you understood your own argument. Readers who chose your article do not need verifying.

**It feels like completion.** Ending on a substantive point can feel abrupt to the writer, because the writer knows there was more they could have said. Readers do not experience it that way.

**It is easy to write.** When you are tired and the piece is nearly done, restating is the lowest-effort way to produce a final paragraph.

The result is a paragraph that consumes the reader's last and most retentive moment on information they already have.

## The test

Delete your final paragraph. Read the piece again.

If nothing is lost, it was padding. Delete another and repeat. Stop when removing one more would actually take something away.

Do this on three of your own drafts and you will find the pattern is consistent — most pieces are one or two paragraphs longer than they need to be, and the surplus is at the end.

## Six endings that earn their place

### 1. The implication

State what follows from your argument that you have not already said.

> Detection cannot be made reliable, for reasons that are structural rather than technical. That means the burden falls on assessment design, which is expensive and slow — and until institutions do that work, individuals absorb the cost of a tool that does not do what its users believe.

The reader now has something they did not have two paragraphs ago. That is what an ending is for.

### 2. The practical next step

Compress the piece into the one thing to actually do.

> The practical version: read your draft aloud. Where you run out of breath, cut. Where three sentences in a row start the same way, rewrite two of them.

This looks like a summary and is not — it is a translation of the argument into an action, which is a different thing from restating it.

### 3. The limitation

Say what your argument does not cover. This builds more credibility than a confident close.

> None of this helps if the underlying draft has no argument. Cutting removes what is not needed; it cannot supply what is missing.

Readers trust writers who mark the edges of their own claims. It also pre-empts the objection they were forming.

### 4. The honest uncertainty

End on what you do not know, when that is genuinely interesting.

> What none of this settles is whether disclosure norms will converge at all, or whether every profession will end up with its own incompatible version. Two years in, there is no sign of the former.

Only use this when the uncertainty is real and specific. Manufactured uncertainty is just hedging in a nicer coat.

### 5. The reframe

One sentence that recasts everything before it. Hard to do well, memorable when it lands.

> Which is the honest summary: the mechanical layer of writing can be assisted, and the layer that makes writing worth reading cannot.

The test is whether the sentence would mean anything on its own. If it would, it is a reframe. If it only makes sense as a recap, it is a summary wearing a costume.

### 6. Stopping

The most underused option. End on your last real point and stop.

If your final substantive paragraph is strong, a conclusion after it dilutes rather than completes. Many good articles simply end, and readers do not experience it as unfinished — they experience it as confident.

## Endings that do not work

**The recap.** "In conclusion, sentence variety is essential for engaging writing. By varying your structure, you can create prose that resonates." Nothing here that the reader did not just read at greater length.

**The call to action that does not fit.** A thoughtful piece about detector bias that ends "so try our tool today!" undoes the credibility the article earned. If a product genuinely helps, mention it where it is relevant, not as a bolted-on close.

**The unearned grandeur.** "As we navigate the ever-evolving landscape of AI, one thing remains clear: the human element is more important than ever." This says nothing and is trying very hard.

**The new argument.** An ending is not the place to introduce a claim you have not supported. If it is important enough to state, it needed a section.

**The apology.** "Of course, this is just one perspective, and there is much more that could be said." Every piece is one perspective. Saying so undercuts what you spent the article establishing.

## On "in conclusion"

Delete it, essentially always.

It announces a structural move the reader can already see — they can tell they are near the end. And it almost always introduces exactly the paragraph this article is arguing against, so the phrase functions as a warning label for padding.

The same goes for "to summarise," "in summary," "all in all," and "at the end of the day." If your final paragraph needs a signpost to explain what it is doing, that is a sign it is not doing much.

## How this fits with openings

Openings and endings fail in mirrored ways. An opening usually has a runway — a paragraph the writer needed to get started, which the reader does not need. An ending usually has a recap — a paragraph the writer needed to feel finished, which the reader also does not need.

The diagnostic is the same for both: delete it and see whether anything is lost. Most drafts survive losing their first paragraph and their last, and read better for it. The opening side is covered in [how to write an opening that earns the next paragraph](/blog/how-to-write-an-opening).

What is left after both deletions is usually the piece you meant to write.
