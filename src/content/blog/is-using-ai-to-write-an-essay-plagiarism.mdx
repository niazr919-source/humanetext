The question comes up in the same form every time, usually with a note of hope in it. If AI text is not copied from anyone, and no source exists to steal from, can it really be plagiarism?

It is a fair question, and the strict answer is often no. It is also the wrong question, and the gap between those two things is where students get into trouble.

## What plagiarism actually means

The standard definition has a specific shape: presenting another person's words, ideas, or work as your own, without attribution. It requires a someone. Somewhere there is an author whose work was taken.

Generated text does not fit that shape cleanly. A model produces a statistically likely sequence of words rather than reproducing a particular person's paragraph. There is no wronged author to credit, and typically no source document a checker could surface.

So under the letter of most traditional plagiarism definitions, submitting AI-written work is not plagiarism.

That was genuinely true, and briefly useful, in about 2023.

## Why that argument no longer helps

Institutions noticed the gap immediately, and closed it. They did not do so by redefining plagiarism — they did it by adding a separate offence.

Read almost any current academic integrity policy and you will find AI addressed under a heading of its own: **unauthorised assistance**, **contract cheating**, **unfair academic advantage**, or an explicitly named AI clause. Some policies also fold generated text into an expanded plagiarism definition, but most keep it distinct precisely because the mechanics differ.

The practical consequence is that arguing "this is not technically plagiarism" concedes the point that matters. Nobody in a misconduct hearing needs it to be plagiarism. They need it to be prohibited, and it is.

Worse, the argument tends to land badly. It reads as someone who has looked for a loophole rather than someone explaining what they did, and hearings are run by people who have heard it before.

## What your policy probably says

Policies vary more than students expect, and reading your own is genuinely worth twenty minutes. Broadly they fall into three groups.

**Prohibited outright.** Any use of generative AI in assessed work is a breach. These are becoming less common because they are hard to enforce and catch legitimate accessibility use.

**Permitted with disclosure.** You may use AI for defined purposes if you declare it, often in a set format at the end of the submission. This is now the most common model.

**Permitted for some stages, not others.** Brainstorming and structural feedback allowed, drafting of submitted prose not. This is the most nuanced and the easiest to breach accidentally.

What almost every policy shares is the distinction between **assistance and substitution** — help with writing you produced, versus a tool producing the writing. Our guide to [using AI writing tools responsibly](/blog/ai-writing-tools-academic-integrity) covers where that line sits in practice.

## The Turnitin confusion

A specific misunderstanding causes a lot of unnecessary panic, so it is worth stating plainly.

Turnitin produces **two separate reports**, and they measure unrelated things.

The **similarity report** matches your text against indexed sources — papers, journals, web pages — and shows you what matched and where. That is the plagiarism check, and it produces evidence you can inspect.

The **AI indicator** estimates how statistically predictable the text is. It produces no source document, because there is nothing to point at. It is an estimate about the shape of the prose.

A high AI score with 0% similarity is completely normal and does not mean plagiarism was found. The reverse happens too. If you are trying to interpret a number you have been shown, [what a Turnitin AI score actually means](/blog/what-a-turnitin-ai-score-means) walks through it.

## Where the real risk sits

Most cases that end badly do not turn on the detector score at all. They turn on the surrounding evidence.

**No drafting history.** A finished document with no earlier versions, no notes, and no research trail is difficult to account for. This is why keeping one matters *before* anything happens — the method is in [how to keep a writing audit trail](/blog/keep-a-writing-audit-trail).

**Inability to discuss your own work.** Many institutions now use a viva or a short conversation when they have concerns. Someone who wrote the essay can say why they structured it that way, what they cut, and which source they found least convincing. Someone who did not, usually cannot.

**A sudden change in standard.** Work that jumps two grade bands, or suddenly adopts a register the student has never used, prompts questions on its own.

**Fabricated citations.** This is the one that decides cases fastest. Models invent plausible-looking references, and a marker who checks one and finds it does not exist has something concrete, unlike a detector score.

## If you are being accused

The definitional question is not your defence. Evidence of process is.

Respond calmly, state plainly what you did and did not use, and provide your drafting history and notes. If you used AI in a permitted way, say exactly what and where — precise disclosure of ordinary assistance is far stronger than a blanket denial that later unravels.

Do not rewrite the work after a challenge. Do not run it through a rewriting tool, ours included, to change the score. Both create a discrepancy between what you submitted and what you defend, and that is worse than the original allegation.

## The honest position

If you want the short version: whether it counts as plagiarism is an interesting question with almost no practical value.

What matters is whether your institution permits it, whether you can show how the work came to exist, and whether you can talk about your own argument. Those three things decide outcomes. The taxonomy does not.

And there is a version of this worth saying plainly, separate from the rules. An essay is an assessment of whether you can think through a problem and set it out. Outsourcing the setting-out means the assessment measures nothing, and the skill it was supposed to build does not get built. That is a real cost, and it lands later, long after the grade.
