This is one of the most confidently misreported topics in SEO, and the underlying document is about four paragraphs long. You can read the whole thing in two minutes.

So rather than summarise the summaries, here is what Google's guidance page on generative AI content actually says, quoted, with the parts people skip.

## The short version

Google does not penalise content for being AI-generated.

Its guidance describes generative AI as something that "can be particularly useful when researching a topic, and to add structure to original content." That is the opening framing, not a grudging concession buried at the bottom.

The one thing it names as a potential violation is narrow, and every word in it is load-bearing.

## The sentence everything turns on

Google says that using these tools "to generate many pages without adding value for users **may violate**" its spam policy on scaled content abuse.

Three qualifiers, all of which get dropped in the retelling:

**"Many pages."** The policy is about scale. One article, or forty, written with care is not what this describes. It targets the operation churning out thousands of pages.

**"Without adding value for users."** This is the actual test, and it has nothing to do with how the text was produced. Pages that add value are fine; pages that do not are not, whoever or whatever wrote them.

**"May violate."** Not *violates*. Conditional.

The named policy is **scaled content abuse**, and that is the only spam policy the page cites. It is worth understanding that this policy predates the current generation of AI tools in spirit — it has always covered mass-produced doorway pages and spun content. Generated text is a new way to trip it, not a new rule.

## What it says about quality

The page's instruction is outcome-focused rather than process-focused. It asks you to focus on "accuracy, quality, and relevance, especially when automatically generating the content."

It then extends that duty somewhere most people do not look: the metadata that can surface in results. Titles, meta descriptions, structured data and image alt text all fall under the same obligation, and it says structured data must comply with the general guidelines and per-feature policies, and should be validated.

That is a genuinely actionable detail. Auto-generating meta descriptions across a site at scale, without checking them, is closer to what the page warns about than writing an article with help.

The page also points at the Search Quality Raters guidelines — specifically section 4.6.5 on scaled content abuse, and 4.6.6 on main content created with "little to no effort, little to no originality, and little to no added value."

Read that second phrase carefully, because it is the real standard: **effort, originality, added value.** None of the three is a statement about tooling.

Google attaches its own caveat, which is routinely omitted when people cite those sections: the rater guidelines "are not a guide to ranking first in Google," and raters' "ratings don't directly influence ranking." They are an explanatory lens, not the penalty mechanism.

## Two things the page does not say

This is where most secondhand accounts go wrong, so it is worth being precise.

**It does not require human review.** The words *human*, *oversight*, *review* and *editor* do not appear on the page. There is no stated requirement for a person in the loop. You will read the opposite constantly — "Google's guidance requires human oversight" — and it is not on that page. The duty is to the output being accurate, high quality and relevant. How you get there is not specified.

**It does not mention E-E-A-T, authors, or bylines.** Not once. Anyone citing this particular URL for a claim about AI content and author bylines is citing something that is not in it. E-E-A-T is real and lives in Google's separate helpful-content documentation; it is simply not what this page is about.

Being accurate about this matters more than it might seem. If you are making decisions about your site based on a rule that does not exist, you are spending effort on the wrong thing.

## Disclosure: optional for Search, required for shopping

There are two separate regimes here and they get conflated constantly.

**For Search, disclosure is advisory.** Under the heading "Give users context", the page says sharing how content was created "can help give your readers more context," and that you should "consider adding information on how your content was created in a way that makes sense for your audience."

*Consider.* No mandate, no prescribed format, no required label.

**For ecommerce through Merchant Center, it is a genuine requirement.** AI-generated images "must contain metadata using the IPTC `DigitalSourceType` `TrainedAlgorithmicMedia` metadata," and AI-generated product data such as title and description attributes "must be specified separately and labeled as AI-generated."

That is a product policy for shopping feeds. It is not a Search ranking rule, and it does not apply to your blog. If you run a store, it applies to you and it is mandatory.

Whether *you* should disclose for reasons other than Google's rules is a separate and more interesting question, covered in [when and how to disclose AI use](/blog/when-and-how-to-disclose-ai-use).

## What actually gets sites into trouble

Not the tool. The pattern.

**Volume without purpose.** Publishing at a rate no editorial process could support, covering keywords rather than questions. This is what scaled content abuse describes, and it was a problem when it was done by underpaid freelancers too.

**Nothing the reader could not get elsewhere.** Content assembled from the top ten results adds nothing to the index. The "little to no originality" phrase is aimed squarely at this.

**Unverified claims.** Generated drafts state false things with total confidence, and fabricated statistics or invented citations will damage a site far faster than any detection question. This is the single biggest practical risk and it gets the least attention.

**Auto-generated metadata nobody read.** Per the page's own warning, and easy to overlook.

## About AI detectors and rankings

A related worry worth closing off: there is no evidence Google uses third-party AI detectors as a ranking input, and it has never said it does. Nothing in this documentation references detection scores.

Those tools estimate how statistically predictable text is and produce false positives on clear, conventional prose — the mechanism is in [how AI content detectors actually work](/blog/how-ai-content-detectors-work). Optimising your writing against a third-party detector score is optimising against a number Google has never claimed to look at.

## The practical position

If you use AI to help research, structure or draft, and you then verify the facts, add something the reader cannot get from the first page of results, and publish at a pace a person could actually sustain — you are on the right side of everything documented above.

If you are producing pages at volume because the marginal cost is near zero, the scale is the problem, and it would be the problem regardless of what wrote them.

The bar has not moved. It is still effort, originality, and added value — which is roughly what it was before any of this, and is also just a description of writing something worth reading.
